# From Manifestation to Practice: Bringing Intention into Learning Design

People often begin a learning experience with a wish: “I want to feel more confident,” “I want to learn to lead a team,” or “I want to start my own project.” Those wishes matter, but they do not automatically become action. A practical learning-design question is how to connect what people care about to a next step they can try and adjust.

In a SELF interview, neuroscientist James Doty offers a practical interpretation of manifestation: keeping a goal in mind may help people notice cues and opportunities related to it. [Read the SELF interview](https://www.self.com/story/neuroscientist-science-behind-manifestation) That idea can be a starting point for reflection, but it should not be presented as “think positively and your goal will come true.” Noticing an opportunity does not mean someone has the resources to act on it, and an unmet goal does not mean a person lacked belief.

A more grounded approach is to connect a wish to an action plan. A meta-analysis of 21 studies with 15,907 participants found a small-to-medium effect on goal attainment when people considered a desired outcome alongside likely obstacles and made “if–then” plans for those obstacles. The authors also noted possible publication bias, which could mean the actual effect is smaller. [Read the meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/)

That distinction matters. The review examined a self-regulation approach that combines imagining a desired outcome, identifying likely obstacles, and planning a response. It did not test visualization of success on its own. In learning design, visualization can help someone clarify a direction; the next step is connecting that direction to a real situation and an action they can take.

## Turn a wish into one concrete practice

In a learning experience or group, four questions can help participants move forward:

1. **Why does this matter to you?** Understand what participants care about before judging whether the goal is ambitious, altruistic, or “right.”
2. **In what situation do you want to take what action?** Turn “be more confident” into an observable behavior, such as asking one question at the next meeting.
3. **What is most likely to get in the way?** Identify barriers involving time, skills, environment, or emotions instead of attributing every difficulty to mindset.
4. **What will you do when that barrier appears?** For example: “If I feel nervous in the meeting, I’ll start by reading the opening question I prepared.”

These questions cannot guarantee success. They can help participants leave with something to test, rather than only a phrase meant to motivate them.

For example, someone who wants to feel more confident in meetings could picture the topic where they want to contribute and anticipate the moment they are most likely to hold back. They could prepare one opening question, then spend a few minutes afterward reflecting: What did I do? How did the conversation change? What would I keep or adjust next time? The goal shifts from “be more confident” to a practice they can observe and refine.

## Make practice reviewable, with other people alongside

A single commitment can easily get crowded out by daily life. Agree on a limited practice period and invite participants to briefly record what they tried, what they noticed, and what they might adjust next time. In a group, sharing attempts and discoveries can create opportunities for feedback and mutual witnessing.

Mutual witnessing does not need to become a leaderboard, streak count, or public rating. Learners can choose how much to share. If someone misses a practice session, they can return at the next opportunity without first facing shame or making up a record. This keeps attention on practice and reflection, rather than perfect performance.

For a group activity, the rhythm can be simple: choose one small action for the week, leave a brief check-in during the practice period, then reflect on the experience together. Participants do not need to grade one another. They can share what they tried, what got in the way, and what they want to do next. The aim is to make experience visible and discussable while leaving room for each person to adjust their pace.

## Support practice and pay attention to the environment

Learning experiences can help participants find direction, break actions into steps, review progress, and adjust goals. But some barriers will not disappear with a mindset shift. Workload, caregiving responsibilities, financial constraints, accessibility needs, and organizational culture can all affect whether someone can put learning into practice.

When discussing manifestation, avoid implying that outcomes depend entirely on a person’s thoughts. More supportive questions include: “What is one small next step? What resources or help would make it possible? How will we know whether this approach is helping?”

When an action repeatedly does not happen, that can also be a signal to review the design. Is the step too large? Is the timing unrealistic? Are tools or support missing from the environment? The answer may be to adjust the task, pace, or surrounding conditions rather than asking the individual to try harder.

Turning a wish into learning does not mean promising a result. It means bringing what matters into real life, trying it, noticing what happens, and choosing a next step. That is one way to move from understanding something to doing it.
