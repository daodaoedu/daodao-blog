Start a weekly learning-group check-in with three questions: What did you actually try? Where did you get stuck? What small step will you take next? Use the answers to arrange support, then return to those steps at the next meeting. Teachers, mentors and community facilitators can adapt this routine for learning activities that run over several weeks.

## Explain how you will use the answers

Tell participants that the purpose is to understand their practice and identify useful support. Agree on who can see the answers, when you will respond and how to share something privately. Do not require public disclosure of personal circumstances.

Carnegie Mellon University's Eberly Center [guidance on designing group projects](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/design/instructionalstrategies/groupprojects/design.html) recommends clear goals and expectations, along with periodic reviews of group processes. The three questions below are this article's suggested facilitation routine, not a validated questionnaire from the center or a guarantee of higher participation.

## Three questions that lead to a next step

### 1. What did you actually try this week?

Ask each participant to choose one attempt and describe what happened. They might share their work on a problem, a question from their reading or something that did not work. Offer a short example of your own; avoid comparing who did the most.

If an answer is simply “I read a little,” ask which section they read and what they remember or do not yet understand.

### 2. Where did you get stuck, and what support would help?

Keep the question specific: At which step did you stop? Would an example, feedback or a practice partner help? “I do not need support right now” is also a valid answer.

For someone who has not started, ask what made starting difficult rather than assuming a lack of commitment. Move sensitive matters to an appropriate private conversation.

### 3. What small step will you try next week?

Ask for an action you can return to, such as choosing one problem and writing its first step, rather than “try harder.” Check whether the plan fits the participant's available time. At the next check-in, discuss the outcome and adjust as needed.

## An example you can adapt

This is a fictional study-group example, not a Daodao customer story or evidence of product results.

| Question | Example answer | A facilitator's possible response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What did you try? | I tried two ratio problems but could not set up the second equation. | Ask them to show their current equation or where they stopped. |
| What support would help? | An example of turning a word problem into an equation. | Offer a relevant example or ask a willing peer to help. |
| What is your next step? | Before we meet again, I will choose one problem and list the known information. | Ask next time what happened and what support is still needed. |

You can put these questions in an existing shared document or message thread. Try one round with the tools you already use before deciding whether you need a dedicated place for activities and responses.

## Follow up on the answers

Identify what needs a response, then decide what belongs in a group discussion and what needs individual support. A submitted answer does not establish understanding. To assess learning, you still need to see a relevant explanation or task.

If nobody responds, check whether the questions are clear, whether answering takes too much work and whether public sharing feels uncomfortable. Try one question, model an answer or offer a private response option. If you cannot respond to everyone, explain what you can cover and when.

Begin the next meeting by returning to participants' chosen steps, within the agreed sharing boundaries. Focus on attempts and adjustments; avoid turning public records into rankings or grounds for blame.
